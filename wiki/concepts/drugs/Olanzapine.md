#olanzapine #antipsychotics #insomnia

**Summary:** Olanzapine (Zyprexa) is an atypical antipsychotic licensed for schizophrenia and bipolar I, used off-label for chemotherapy nausea, as an appetite stimulant, and as a sedative for insomnia. It is among the most effective antipsychotics for keeping people on treatment, and the worst of the class for weight gain and metabolic damage. Generic since 2011.

**Evidence:** *Strong evidence* for symptom control in schizophrenia and acute mania; *Strong evidence* for weight gain and metabolic harm as the dose-limiting problem; *No real evidence* for primary insomnia, and guidelines recommend against it there. Dose: 2.5-20mg oral; usual clinical range 10-20mg/day. **Weight gain is only weakly dose-related** — cutting to 5mg reduces it by roughly a quarter, not by most.

The corrected figures are used below; see [[olanzapine-wikipedia#Where the source is wrong]].

## What it treats

*Strong evidence* for the licensed indications. FDA-approved for schizophrenia; acute manic or mixed episodes in bipolar I and maintenance; as an adjunct to lithium or valproate in mania; with fluoxetine (Symbyax) for bipolar depression and treatment-resistant depression; and with samidorphan (Lybalvi) for schizophrenia and bipolar I. UK and Australian approvals are narrower — schizophrenia, moderate-to-severe mania, and short-term acute mania.

NICE lists it as a first-line therapy for acute mania, alongside aripiprazole, haloperidol, quetiapine and risperidone. With fluoxetine it is first-line for acute bipolar depression; alone it is second-line for bipolar maintenance.

A 2014 network meta-analysis put olanzapine-plus-fluoxetine top of nine treatments for bipolar depression.

Off-label: chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting, and as an appetite stimulant in cancer patients.

### Maintenance data is weaker than it looks

*Mixed evidence*. Trials of maintenance therapy are hard to read because most participants leave early.

> The usefulness of maintenance therapy, however, is difficult to determine, as more than half of people in trials quit before the 6-week completion date.

Against that, the NIMH CATIE trial found olanzapine controlled symptoms best precisely because patients stayed on it longer than the comparators — effectiveness driven by tolerability of the psychiatric effects, not the metabolic ones.

## Sleep and insomnia

*Some evidence* that it improves sleep continuity in people who already have a psychiatric reason to take it. *No real evidence* in primary insomnia, where guidelines recommend against antipsychotics. It is not licensed for insomnia in any jurisdiction. #insomnia #sleep

Summary of the trade: olanzapine reliably knocks people out and, unusually for a sedative, the acute data show it deepening sleep rather than only flattening it. The trouble is that the effect is best documented in a 9-person single-dose study, the one placebo-controlled trial in patients missed its primary sleep endpoint, there is no controlled evidence in primary insomnia at all, and the cost is the worst metabolic profile of any antipsychotic in use — paid nightly, for a symptom.

### Acute effect in healthy volunteers: large SWS increase

*Good evidence, small study.* Sharpley et al. 2000 (*Biological Psychiatry*; placebo-controlled, double-blind, cross-over; n=9 healthy males; single evening doses of 5mg and 10mg).

> Compared to placebo, the 5-mg and 10-mg doses of olanzapine significantly increased SWS, sleep continuity measures, and subjective sleep quality. In addition, 10 mg of olanzapine suppressed rapid eye movement (REM) sleep and increased REM sleep latency.

> Olanzapine (5 mg and 10 mg) produced substantial (59.1% and 83.3%) and highly significant dose-related increases in SWS in humans probably via blockade of brain 5-HT2C receptors.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10704958/

This single study is the source of nearly every "olanzapine increases slow-wave sleep" claim in the literature. Note the REM suppression at 10mg — the same direction as antihistamines and most antidepressants, see [[wiki/concepts/sleep/Sleep#Antihistamines|Sleep#Antihistamines]].

### The placebo-controlled trial in patients missed its primary endpoint

*Mixed evidence.* Lazowski et al. 2014 (*BMC Psychiatry*; prospective, double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled; n=25 analysed, 15 olanzapine / 10 placebo; unipolar and bipolar depression; olanzapine added to existing medication, mean dose 6.67mg, range 5-10mg; 28-31 days; home polysomnography). SWS was the pre-specified primary outcome and it was null.

> Olanzapine augmentation treatment generally did not improve SWS but did improve sleep continuity and depression.

> Latency to but not duration of SWS was found to significantly differ between olanzapine- and placebo-treated participants (Hedge's g: 0.97, 0.13 respectively).

Sleep continuity, all secondary outcomes (olanzapine baseline → day 28-31, placebo in brackets): total sleep time 326 → 363 min [315 → 289], sleep efficiency 71% → 83% [67% → 63%], total wake time 135 → 80 min [153 → 169], latency to sleep 69 → 41 min [76 → 92]. SWS as a percentage of total sleep time did not move: 10% → 10% [17% → 17%].

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4223523/

Caveats the authors state: the trial was stopped early for failure to recruit (25 analysed against a target of 30-40), nearly every participant was on other psychotropics, and there were many secondary outcomes against a small sample. Three of fifteen olanzapine patients left the trial early with worsening mood. Weight over the same month: olanzapine 178 → 182 lbs, placebo 185 → 185 lbs (Table 1) — roughly 2 kg in four weeks, consistent with [[wiki/concepts/drugs/Olanzapine#Weight gain and metabolic effects|the metabolic section below]].

The contrast with the healthy-volunteer result matters: a large acute SWS increase in nine drug-free young men did not survive four weeks in medicated patients.

### Nothing usable in primary insomnia

*No real evidence.* There is no randomised placebo-controlled trial of olanzapine in primary insomnia. What exists is small, uncontrolled and mostly subjective.

The longest follow-up is uncontrolled. Sleep Science 2021 (Kermanshah, Iran; one-year follow-up, n=40, olanzapine n=22 vs quetiapine n=18, no placebo arm), using the PSQI questionnaire plus five nights of actigraphy.

> Results showed total PSQI score improved significantly in both groups (p<0.05), while the actigraphy results showed only significant improvement in sleep quality parameters in the quetiapine group (p<0.05).

https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8157779/

The authors' own conclusion — "Two atypical antipsychotics drugs of olanzapine and quetiapine have long-term efficacy in managing chronic insomnia" — overstates their data. For olanzapine specifically, only the self-report measure moved; the objective one did not. With no placebo arm and a year of expectancy, a subjective-only improvement is close to uninformative.

The other cited trial is in paradoxical insomnia, not ordinary insomnia, and is subjective after baseline. Neurosciences (Riyadh) 2013: n=29 randomised to olanzapine 10mg or risperidone 4mg for 8 weeks, actigraphy at baseline only, PSQI as the outcome; olanzapine beat risperidone on PSQI.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23291800/

### Does 5mg change the answer?

*Some improvement, not a reprieve.* 5mg is meaningfully better than 10-20mg and gets most of the sleep effect, but it is not a "low dose" in the sense that 25mg quetiapine is, and the weight penalty is only weakly dose-related. #dose

**5mg is a full therapeutic antipsychotic dose, not a sub-threshold one.** This is the first thing that separates it from the quetiapine case. Kapur et al. 1998 (*American Journal of Psychiatry*; PET, [11C]raclopride and [18F]setoperone; n=12 patients randomised to 5, 10, 15 or 20mg/day plus 3 on 30-40mg).

> Olanzapine induced near saturation of the 5-HT2 receptors, even at 5 mg/day. Its D2 occupancy increased with dose: patients taking 5-20 mg/day showed 43%-80% D2 occupancy

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9659858/

Mean D2 occupancy at 5mg was about 60%, against the ~65% usually taken as the antipsychotic threshold, and 5-HT2 is already near-saturated. Contrast quetiapine 25mg, which produces negligible D2 occupancy — genuinely a different drug at sleep doses. Olanzapine 5mg is the licensed starting dose for schizophrenia, not a fraction of it. In a 9,565-patient registry the "low-dose" cutoff was **<2.5mg**; 5mg sat in the regular-dose group.

**Weight gain barely tracks dose.** This is the finding that matters most for the question. Schoretsanitis et al. 2022 (*Basic & Clinical Pharmacology & Toxicology*; prospective cohort, n=392, median dose 10mg/day, IQR 5.0-10.0, median follow-up 40 days).

> weight gain was not associated with olanzapine dose (p = 0.61) although it was larger for doses >10 mg/day versus ≤10 mg/day (2.54 ± 5.55 vs. 1.61 ± 4.51% respectively, p = 0.01)

> The lack of major dose-dependent patterns for weight gain emphasizes that olanzapine-treated patients are at weight gain risk regardless of the dose.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35150056/

Halving the dose did not halve the weight gain; ≤10mg still produced 1.61%.

**Low dose does not spare the lipid and blood pressure effects — it may be worse for them.** From the same cohort, an awkward result for the dose-sparing argument:

> Lower doses were associated with increase in total and HDL cholesterol and systolic and diastolic blood pressure (p < 0.05), whereas higher doses were associated with glucose increases (p = 0.01)

*Likely:* this is confounded — lower doses go to older, more medically complex or more metabolically fragile patients — rather than a genuine inverse dose effect. But it does mean the metabolic harm cannot be assumed to scale down cleanly.

**The dose-response curve does rise, so 5mg is genuinely better than 20mg.** Sabé et al. 2022 (*Schizophrenia Bulletin*; dose-response meta-analysis, 97 studies, 36,326 participants; olanzapine arm 16 studies, n=3,575, doses 1-40mg/day, median duration 6 weeks) found olanzapine among the minority of antipsychotics whose weight curve never flattens.

> The dose-response curve did not plateau at the highest examined dose (P-value < .01) (40mg/d with a corresponding MD = 3.62kg). Nevertheless, the slope of the curve was smaller beyond 10 mg/d

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9077426/

Read together with the cohort above: the curve climbs steeply from zero and then flattens out, so the first 5mg buys most of the weight penalty and each further 5mg adds less. Dose-sparing helps at the margin; it does not move you to a different risk class. *Unverified:* the meta-analysis reports a figure but no extractable mean difference for 5mg specifically, so no number is given here.

The authors' explanation is the mechanism already on this page — the appetite-driving targets are high-affinity and saturate early:

> it is conceivable that dose-response curves of antipsychotics involving these receptors, such as clozapine and olanzapine, don't reach a plateau

**Long-run figures at ≤5mg are not small.** At four years, women on >5mg gained ~6.1 kg against ~4.4 kg on ≤5mg — a 28% reduction for a 4-fold dose cut. And the one placebo-controlled sleep trial ran at a mean of 6.67mg (range 5-10mg): the olanzapine group put on roughly 2 kg in four weeks. See [[wiki/concepts/drugs/Olanzapine#The placebo-controlled trial in patients missed its primary endpoint|the trial above]].

**Going much lower may be a different story.** A Korean registry (*Journal of Personalized Medicine* 2025; retrospective cohort, 9,565 olanzapine patients 2002-2023, 1,629 on **<2.5mg**) found substantially reduced metabolic events against regular doses: diabetes HR 0.32 (0.17-0.62), dyslipidaemia HR 0.59 (0.42-0.82). Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular differences were not significant.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40863442/

*Likely confounded:* patients put on under 2.5mg differ systematically from those on 10-20mg, and the study is retrospective with no adjustment described for indication. It suggests the harm does fall away eventually, but at a quarter of 5mg or less, and it says nothing about whether such a dose does anything for sleep.

**What 5mg does buy on the sleep side.** In [[wiki/concepts/drugs/Olanzapine#Acute effect in healthy volunteers: large SWS increase|Sharpley et al. 2000]], 5mg produced a 59.1% increase in slow-wave sleep against 83.3% at 10mg — most of the effect — and the REM suppression appeared **only at 10mg**. So on that one study, 5mg is the better sleep dose as well as the safer one: nearly all the deepening, none of the REM cost.

**Net.** 5mg is the right dose *if* olanzapine is being taken at all, and it is a real improvement on 10-20mg. It is not a dose at which the objection goes away. There is still no evidence in primary insomnia at any dose, guidelines still recommend against the class, the elderly black box does not have a dose exemption, and the weight and metabolic risk is reduced by something like a quarter to a third rather than eliminated. Someone taking 5mg nightly for sleep alone is accepting most of the drug's metabolic cost for an indication no trial supports.

### Guidelines recommend against antipsychotics for insomnia

*Strong recommendation, weak evidence base* — the guidelines advise against precisely because there are no trials, not because trials came out negative.

VA/DoD 2025:

> weak recommendation against using antipsychotic drugs, including quetiapine, for the treatment of chronic insomnia disorder

European insomnia guideline 2023:

> does not recommend quetiapine and other antipsychotics for the treatment of insomnia

https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK619998/

Both statements name quetiapine, which is the far more commonly misprescribed one; the European wording extends to the class. *Unverified:* neither guideline appears to assess olanzapine for insomnia separately, because there is nothing to assess.

### Why it is a bad trade for sleep specifically

The harms are the same as at psychiatric doses but the indication is much weaker.

- Weight and metabolic damage, the worst of the class, and dose-dependent rather than absent at low doses. See [[wiki/concepts/drugs/Olanzapine#Weight gain and metabolic effects|Weight gain and metabolic effects]].
- *Don't take* in older people with dementia-related symptoms — black box for increased death, and this is the exact off-label use Eli Lilly pleaded guilty to promoting. See [[wiki/concepts/drugs/Olanzapine#Elderly and dementia — black box|Elderly and dementia]].
- It can cause the sleep problem it is given for. Restless legs syndrome and periodic limb movements are a recognised but under-recognised olanzapine effect, and are dose-related. Kang et al. 2009 (*Journal of Psychopharmacology*), five cases — case series, low-quality evidence.

> Antipsychotic-induced RLS and PLMS are not well-recognized side effects of antipsychotics, with the symptoms often misdiagnosed as psychotic agitation.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18562412/

- Next-day sedation, plus the general point on [[wiki/concepts/sleep/Sleep#Sleeping pills|Sleep#Sleeping pills]] that sedation is not the same state as sleep.
- Insomnia is itself a listed withdrawal symptom, so stopping is not free. See [[wiki/concepts/drugs/Olanzapine#Discontinuation|Discontinuation]].

**Compared with quetiapine (Seroquel)**, the antipsychotic actually prescribed as a sleeping pill: most of the above carries over, but olanzapine wins the two comparisons that exist. Quetiapine does not increase slow-wave sleep (it adds stage 2 light sleep), and it failed the one RCT in primary insomnia that olanzapine has never had. See [[wiki/concepts/drugs/Quetiapine#How it compares with olanzapine for sleep|Quetiapine#How it compares with olanzapine for sleep]].

**Where it is defensible:** as a single drug doing two jobs when the person needs an antipsychotic anyway — depression or bipolar depression with severe insomnia, or the palliative setting where nausea, anorexia, anxiety and insomnia are being treated together and long-term metabolic harm is not the relevant clock. Not as a sleeping pill in an otherwise well person.

## Weight gain and metabolic effects

*Strong evidence* — this is the drug's defining problem. #metabolic

Olanzapine causes more weight gain than any other antipsychotic in common use. Leucht et al. 2013 (*Lancet*, multiple-treatments meta-analysis of 15 antipsychotics) ranks it worst, at SMD 0.74. *Unverified:* the effect size itself is paywalled and was not read directly; the ranking is consistent with everything else here.

Magnitudes from trial and cohort data, rather than from the source article:

- ~5.1 kg at 24 weeks (ENLIGHTEN-2 olanzapine arm, 6.59% mean weight change).
- 11.4 kg in studies longer than 13 weeks, versus 5.5 kg in studies of 13 weeks or less.
- Over a median 2.5-3 years, 22% of patients gained 10-20 kg and 9% gained more than 20 kg.
- Only weakly dose-dependent. At four years, women on >5mg gained ~6.1 kg versus ~4.4 kg on ≤5mg; a prospective cohort (n=392) found no association with dose at all (p=0.61). See [[wiki/concepts/drugs/Olanzapine#Does 5mg change the answer?|Does 5mg change the answer?]].

https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6947810/

The FDA requires a hyperglycaemia and diabetes warning on all atypical antipsychotics. Olanzapine raises diabetes risk more than risperidone, may reduce insulin sensitivity, and raises triglycerides. Metabolic changes have been reported without weight gain, so it is not purely a consequence of eating more.

> These effects may be related to the drugs' ability to induce weight gain, although some reports have been made of metabolic changes in the absence of weight gain.

**Mechanism.** Antagonism at H1 histamine, M3 muscarinic and D2 dopamine receptors is associated with weight gain and appetite stimulation; 5-HT2C antagonism alone is not. M3 antagonism is the candidate for the diabetogenic effect specifically.

### Samidorphan (Lybalvi) reduces but does not remove it

*Good evidence, modest effect*. ENLIGHTEN-2, 24-week phase 3: least-squares mean weight change 4.21% on olanzapine/samidorphan versus 6.59% on olanzapine, a difference of -2.38% (p=0.003). Weight gain over 10% of baseline occurred in 17.8% versus 29.8%. Antipsychotic efficacy was similar.

https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2020.19121279

## Other adverse effects

Common: sedation, dizziness, constipation, dry mouth, akathisia. Sedation comes from H1 antagonism; dry mouth and constipation from muscarinic antagonism.

*Weak evidence* of extrapyramidal problems relative to first-generation drugs — infrequent to rare, attributed to higher 5-HT2A than D2 affinity — but tremor and rigidity do occur, and anticholinergic action masks EPS without protecting against tardive dyskinesia.

Prolactin rises early and dose-dependently but usually transiently: about half of patients on olanzapine versus over 90% on risperidone.

Serious but uncommon: neuroleptic malignant syndrome, seizures, tardive dyskinesia, orthostatic hypotension, thermoregulatory failure under heat or exertion.

### Elderly and dementia — black box

*Don't take* in dementia-related psychosis. The UK Committee on the Safety of Medicines warned in 2004 against olanzapine and risperidone in elderly dementia patients, citing stroke risk; the US carries a black box warning for increased death. A BBC investigation in June 2008 found British doctors were widely ignoring the advice. This is the same use Eli Lilly pleaded guilty to promoting (below).

### Post-injection delirium/sedation syndrome

Specific to the long-acting injectable (olanzapine pamoate), at an estimated 0.07% of administrations, and not seen with other second-generation long-acting injectables such as paliperidone palmitate. Delirium and sedation in 83% of cases. Thought to be caused by accidental absorption into the bloodstream rather than slow release from muscle. The FDA requires 3 hours of observation after injection.

### Pregnancy and breastfeeding

*Mixed evidence*. Olanzapine has the highest placental exposure of any atypical antipsychotic; available evidence suggests it is safe in pregnancy, but is not strong enough to say so confidently. Weight gain may raise offspring risk of neural tube defects. Breastfeeding is advised against — infant exposure is about 1.8% of the mother's in one study.

### Discontinuation

The BNF recommends gradual withdrawal. Nausea, vomiting and appetite loss are the common withdrawal symptoms; also restlessness, sweating, insomnia. *Some evidence* that stopping antipsychotics can itself produce psychosis as a temporary withdrawal effect, distinct from relapse of the underlying condition.

### Overdose

Moderately toxic — worse than quetiapine, aripiprazole and SSRIs, better than MAOIs and tricyclics. No specific antidote.

> Death has been reported after an acute overdose of 450 mg, but also survival after an acute overdose of 2000 mg.

## Interactions

Metabolised mainly by CYP1A2, minor CYP2D6. **Tobacco smoke induces CYP1A2 and significantly increases clearance** — the practical consequence being that starting or stopping smoking moves blood levels. Carbamazepine cut the concentration/dose ratio by 33%; ritonavir also reduces exposure. CYP1A2 inhibitors (ciprofloxacin, fluvoxamine) reduce clearance; probenecid raises exposure. Fluoxetine, despite inhibiting CYP2D6, has no clinically significant effect.

Clearance is about 25% lower in women than men, and 26% higher in self-identified African Americans or Blacks. No difference between Caucasian, Chinese or Japanese groups.

Olanzapine has the highest P-glycoprotein affinity of any second-generation antipsychotic *in vitro*, so anything else interacting with P-gp — including common foods — can shift how much reaches the brain.

Used as a "trip killer" to block the effects of serotonergic psychedelics such as psilocybin and LSD. See [[Psycedelics]]. #tripkiller

## Eli Lilly litigation

*Strong evidence* — court record, not allegation. #pharma

Lilly settled roughly 8,000 diabetes-related suits for $700m in 2006 and about 18,000 more for $500m in early 2007, reaching $1.2bn. A December 2006 *New York Times* piece based on leaked internal documents concluded the company had deliberately downplayed side effects; Lilly said the documents were cherry-picked. *The Times* of London reported that Lilly had internally treated drug-induced obesity as a threat to sales as early as 1998.

> quite impressed by the magnitude of weight gain on olanzapine and implications for glucose

— senior Lilly research physician Robert Baker, 9 October 2000, on an academic advisory board's reaction.

In January 2009 Lilly pleaded guilty to a federal criminal misdemeanour for off-label marketing and agreed to pay $1.415bn, including a $515m criminal fine — at the time the largest criminal fine imposed on a single corporation in any US prosecution.

> Eli Lilly admits that between September 1999 and March 31, 2001, the company promoted Zyprexa in elderly populations as a treatment for dementia, including Alzheimer's dementia.

https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/eli-lilly-and-company-agrees-pay-1415-billion-resolve-allegations-label-promotion-zyprexa

The document leak itself became litigation: the papers reached the *Times* via lawyer Jim Gottstein from expert consultant David Egilman, were spread on file-sharing networks by Will Hall and others in the psychiatric survivors movement, and Judge Jack B. Weinstein granted Lilly a protection order while criticising all three. Egilman paid Lilly $100,000 in 2007 to end a threatened contempt charge. Gottstein's account is *The Zyprexa Papers* (2021) — a participant's account, not a neutral one.

## Research uses

*Some evidence* in anorexia nervosa. Attia et al. 2019 (RCT, n=152, 16 weeks, adult outpatients): weight gain 6.7 kg on olanzapine versus 4.2 kg on placebo, with no improvement in obsessionality or cognitive symptoms — the weight goes on, the illness does not shift.

*Weak evidence* in cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome. The source claims "a high success rate comparable to haloperidol"; this is unreferenced in the source and does not hold. The olanzapine-versus-haloperidol head-to-head is in *chemotherapy*-induced nausea, and the haloperidol evidence in CHS (HaVOC trial) compared it with ondansetron, not olanzapine. Olanzapine in CHS rests on about six case reports.

## Debunks

**It's not true that olanzapine typically causes 23-45 kg of weight gain long-term.** That figure appears in the source article and is not supported. See the callout above and [[olanzapine-wikipedia#Where the source is wrong]].

## History and status

Patented 1991, approved in the US September 1996, generic 2011. Discovered while looking for a clozapine analogue that would not need haematological monitoring — a thiophene isostere of one of clozapine's phenyl rings. On the WHO List of Essential Medicines. 167th most prescribed medication in the US in 2023. Zyprexa sales peaked at $4.7bn worldwide in 2008.

Carcinogenic in chronic exposure in female mice and rats (liver and mammary tumours), not in males. No human signal recorded in the source.
