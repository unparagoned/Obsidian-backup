#olanzapine #antipsychotics

**Summary:** Olanzapine (Zyprexa) is an atypical antipsychotic licensed for schizophrenia and bipolar I, used off-label for chemotherapy nausea and as an appetite stimulant. It is among the most effective antipsychotics for keeping people on treatment, and the worst of the class for weight gain and metabolic damage. Generic since 2011.

**Evidence:** *Strong evidence* for symptom control in schizophrenia and acute mania; *Strong evidence* for weight gain and metabolic harm as the dose-limiting problem. Dose: 2.5-20mg oral; usual clinical range 10-20mg/day.

Related: [[Antipsychotics]] · [[Depression]] · [[Mental health]] · [[Mounjaro tirzepatide#Antipsychotics]] · [[Dementia alzheimer]] · [[Pharmaceutical industry]] · [[olanzapine-wikipedia|Source: Wikipedia article]]

> [!warning] Conflicting evidence
> The source article's own figures for long-term weight gain (23-45 kg typical) are far above what the trial and cohort literature supports (~11 kg beyond 13 weeks; >20 kg in 9% of patients over 2.5-3 years). The corrected figures are used below; see [[olanzapine-wikipedia#Where the source is wrong]].

## What it treats

*Strong evidence* for the licensed indications. FDA-approved for schizophrenia; acute manic or mixed episodes in bipolar I and maintenance; as an adjunct to lithium or valproate in mania; with fluoxetine (Symbyax) for bipolar depression and treatment-resistant depression; and with samidorphan (Lybalvi) for schizophrenia and bipolar I. UK and Australian approvals are narrower — schizophrenia, moderate-to-severe mania, and short-term acute mania.

NICE lists it as a first-line therapy for acute mania, alongside aripiprazole, haloperidol, quetiapine and risperidone. With fluoxetine it is first-line for acute bipolar depression; alone it is second-line for bipolar maintenance.

A 2014 network meta-analysis put olanzapine-plus-fluoxetine top of nine treatments for bipolar depression.

Off-label: chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting, and as an appetite stimulant in cancer patients.

### Maintenance data is weaker than it looks

*Mixed evidence*. Trials of maintenance therapy are hard to read because most participants leave early.

> The usefulness of maintenance therapy, however, is difficult to determine, as more than half of people in trials quit before the 6-week completion date.

Against that, the NIMH CATIE trial found olanzapine controlled symptoms best precisely because patients stayed on it longer than the comparators — effectiveness driven by tolerability of the psychiatric effects, not the metabolic ones.

## Weight gain and metabolic effects

*Strong evidence* — this is the drug's defining problem. #metabolic

Olanzapine causes more weight gain than any other antipsychotic in common use. Leucht et al. 2013 (*Lancet*, multiple-treatments meta-analysis of 15 antipsychotics) ranks it worst, at SMD 0.74. *Unverified:* the effect size itself is paywalled and was not read directly; the ranking is consistent with everything else here.

Magnitudes from trial and cohort data, rather than from the source article:

- ~5.1 kg at 24 weeks (ENLIGHTEN-2 olanzapine arm, 6.59% mean weight change).
- 11.4 kg in studies longer than 13 weeks, versus 5.5 kg in studies of 13 weeks or less.
- Over a median 2.5-3 years, 22% of patients gained 10-20 kg and 9% gained more than 20 kg.
- Dose-dependent. At four years, women on >5mg gained ~6.1 kg versus ~4.4 kg on ≤5mg.

https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6947810/

The FDA requires a hyperglycaemia and diabetes warning on all atypical antipsychotics. Olanzapine raises diabetes risk more than risperidone, may reduce insulin sensitivity, and raises triglycerides. Metabolic changes have been reported without weight gain, so it is not purely a consequence of eating more.

> These effects may be related to the drugs' ability to induce weight gain, although some reports have been made of metabolic changes in the absence of weight gain.

**Mechanism.** Antagonism at H1 histamine, M3 muscarinic and D2 dopamine receptors is associated with weight gain and appetite stimulation; 5-HT2C antagonism alone is not. M3 antagonism is the candidate for the diabetogenic effect specifically.

### Samidorphan (Lybalvi) reduces but does not remove it

*Good evidence, modest effect*. ENLIGHTEN-2, 24-week phase 3: least-squares mean weight change 4.21% on olanzapine/samidorphan versus 6.59% on olanzapine, a difference of -2.38% (p=0.003). Weight gain over 10% of baseline occurred in 17.8% versus 29.8%. Antipsychotic efficacy was similar.

https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2020.19121279

## Other adverse effects

Common: sedation, dizziness, constipation, dry mouth, akathisia. Sedation comes from H1 antagonism; dry mouth and constipation from muscarinic antagonism.

*Weak evidence* of extrapyramidal problems relative to first-generation drugs — infrequent to rare, attributed to higher 5-HT2A than D2 affinity — but tremor and rigidity do occur, and anticholinergic action masks EPS without protecting against tardive dyskinesia.

Prolactin rises early and dose-dependently but usually transiently: about half of patients on olanzapine versus over 90% on risperidone.

Serious but uncommon: neuroleptic malignant syndrome, seizures, tardive dyskinesia, orthostatic hypotension, thermoregulatory failure under heat or exertion.

### Elderly and dementia — black box

*Don't take* in dementia-related psychosis. The UK Committee on the Safety of Medicines warned in 2004 against olanzapine and risperidone in elderly dementia patients, citing stroke risk; the US carries a black box warning for increased death. A BBC investigation in June 2008 found British doctors were widely ignoring the advice. This is the same use Eli Lilly pleaded guilty to promoting (below).

### Post-injection delirium/sedation syndrome

Specific to the long-acting injectable (olanzapine pamoate), at an estimated 0.07% of administrations, and not seen with other second-generation long-acting injectables such as paliperidone palmitate. Delirium and sedation in 83% of cases. Thought to be caused by accidental absorption into the bloodstream rather than slow release from muscle. The FDA requires 3 hours of observation after injection.

### Pregnancy and breastfeeding

*Mixed evidence*. Olanzapine has the highest placental exposure of any atypical antipsychotic; available evidence suggests it is safe in pregnancy, but is not strong enough to say so confidently. Weight gain may raise offspring risk of neural tube defects. Breastfeeding is advised against — infant exposure is about 1.8% of the mother's in one study.

### Discontinuation

The BNF recommends gradual withdrawal. Nausea, vomiting and appetite loss are the common withdrawal symptoms; also restlessness, sweating, insomnia. *Some evidence* that stopping antipsychotics can itself produce psychosis as a temporary withdrawal effect, distinct from relapse of the underlying condition.

### Overdose

Moderately toxic — worse than quetiapine, aripiprazole and SSRIs, better than MAOIs and tricyclics. No specific antidote.

> Death has been reported after an acute overdose of 450 mg, but also survival after an acute overdose of 2000 mg.

## Interactions

Metabolised mainly by CYP1A2, minor CYP2D6. **Tobacco smoke induces CYP1A2 and significantly increases clearance** — the practical consequence being that starting or stopping smoking moves blood levels. Carbamazepine cut the concentration/dose ratio by 33%; ritonavir also reduces exposure. CYP1A2 inhibitors (ciprofloxacin, fluvoxamine) reduce clearance; probenecid raises exposure. Fluoxetine, despite inhibiting CYP2D6, has no clinically significant effect.

Clearance is about 25% lower in women than men, and 26% higher in self-identified African Americans or Blacks. No difference between Caucasian, Chinese or Japanese groups.

Olanzapine has the highest P-glycoprotein affinity of any second-generation antipsychotic *in vitro*, so anything else interacting with P-gp — including common foods — can shift how much reaches the brain.

Used as a "trip killer" to block the effects of serotonergic psychedelics such as psilocybin and LSD. See [[Psycedelics]]. #tripkiller

## Eli Lilly litigation

*Strong evidence* — court record, not allegation. #pharma

Lilly settled roughly 8,000 diabetes-related suits for $700m in 2006 and about 18,000 more for $500m in early 2007, reaching $1.2bn. A December 2006 *New York Times* piece based on leaked internal documents concluded the company had deliberately downplayed side effects; Lilly said the documents were cherry-picked. *The Times* of London reported that Lilly had internally treated drug-induced obesity as a threat to sales as early as 1998.

> quite impressed by the magnitude of weight gain on olanzapine and implications for glucose

— senior Lilly research physician Robert Baker, 9 October 2000, on an academic advisory board's reaction.

In January 2009 Lilly pleaded guilty to a federal criminal misdemeanour for off-label marketing and agreed to pay $1.415bn, including a $515m criminal fine — at the time the largest criminal fine imposed on a single corporation in any US prosecution.

> Eli Lilly admits that between September 1999 and March 31, 2001, the company promoted Zyprexa in elderly populations as a treatment for dementia, including Alzheimer's dementia.

https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/eli-lilly-and-company-agrees-pay-1415-billion-resolve-allegations-label-promotion-zyprexa

The document leak itself became litigation: the papers reached the *Times* via lawyer Jim Gottstein from expert consultant David Egilman, were spread on file-sharing networks by Will Hall and others in the psychiatric survivors movement, and Judge Jack B. Weinstein granted Lilly a protection order while criticising all three. Egilman paid Lilly $100,000 in 2007 to end a threatened contempt charge. Gottstein's account is *The Zyprexa Papers* (2021) — a participant's account, not a neutral one.

## Research uses

*Some evidence* in anorexia nervosa. Attia et al. 2019 (RCT, n=152, 16 weeks, adult outpatients): weight gain 6.7 kg on olanzapine versus 4.2 kg on placebo, with no improvement in obsessionality or cognitive symptoms — the weight goes on, the illness does not shift.

*Weak evidence* in cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome. The source claims "a high success rate comparable to haloperidol"; this is unreferenced in the source and does not hold. The olanzapine-versus-haloperidol head-to-head is in *chemotherapy*-induced nausea, and the haloperidol evidence in CHS (HaVOC trial) compared it with ondansetron, not olanzapine. Olanzapine in CHS rests on about six case reports.

## Debunks

**It's not true that olanzapine typically causes 23-45 kg of weight gain long-term.** That figure appears in the source article and is not supported. See the callout above and [[olanzapine-wikipedia#Where the source is wrong]].

## History and status

Patented 1991, approved in the US September 1996, generic 2011. Discovered while looking for a clozapine analogue that would not need haematological monitoring — a thiophene isostere of one of clozapine's phenyl rings. On the WHO List of Essential Medicines. 167th most prescribed medication in the US in 2023. Zyprexa sales peaked at $4.7bn worldwide in 2008.

Carcinogenic in chronic exposure in female mice and rats (liver and mammary tumours), not in males. No human signal recorded in the source.
