#quetiapine #seroquel #antipsychotics #insomnia

**Summary:** Quetiapine (Seroquel) is the antipsychotic actually being handed out as a sleeping pill. Most of the argument against olanzapine for sleep carries over, but two things differ and both make quetiapine's case *worse*: it does not deepen sleep the way olanzapine does — it just adds light sleep — and unlike olanzapine it has been tested in primary insomnia and failed.

**Evidence:** *No real evidence* in primary insomnia — one small RCT, null. *Some evidence* it shortens sleep onset and adds total sleep time acutely. *Don't take* as a routine sleeping pill, particularly over 65. Off-label sleep dose: 25-100mg nightly, against a licensed antipsychotic range of 150-800mg/day.

Related: [[Antipsychotics]] · [[wiki/concepts/drugs/Olanzapine#Sleep and insomnia|Olanzapine#Sleep and insomnia]] · [[wiki/concepts/sleep/Sleep#Antipsychotics - major tranquillisers - mood stabilisers|Sleep#Antipsychotics]] · [[Depression]] · [[Dementia alzheimer]] · [[Pharmaceutical industry]]

> [!warning] Conflicting evidence
> Subjective and objective measures disagree, consistently and in the same direction. Patients report sleeping better; polysomnography and actigraphy mostly do not confirm it, and the one randomised trial in primary insomnia was null on every primary outcome. See [[wiki/concepts/drugs/Quetiapine#The one RCT in primary insomnia was null|the RCT]] and [[wiki/concepts/drugs/Quetiapine#It does not deepen sleep — it adds stage 2|the architecture data]].

## How it compares with olanzapine for sleep

The short answer to "does the olanzapine case carry over": mostly yes, and where it diverges quetiapine comes off worse.

| | Olanzapine | Quetiapine |
|---|---|---|
| Slow-wave sleep | Large acute increase (59-83%, n=9) | **No increase** — extra stage 2 instead |
| RCT in primary insomnia | None exists | One, n=13 completers, **null** |
| Sleep continuity in depression | Improved (RCT) | **No change** (single-blind study) |
| Weight gain | Worst of the class, ~11 kg long-term | Less, but real at 25-100mg |
| Is the sleep dose sub-therapeutic? | **No** — 5mg is a full antipsychotic dose (~60% D2) | Yes — 25mg gives negligible D2 occupancy |
| Guidelines | Advised against as part of the class | **Named explicitly** in both guidelines |
| Off-label marketing fine | $1.415bn, for **dementia** | $520m, for **sleeplessness** |
| Actually used for sleep | Rarely | Constantly |
| Elderly black box | Yes | Yes |
| Can cause RLS/PLMS | Yes | Yes, dose-related |

One asymmetry runs the other way: quetiapine's sleep dose really is sub-therapeutic as an antipsychotic, whereas olanzapine 5mg is a full antipsychotic dose that near-saturates 5-HT2 and gives ~60% D2 occupancy. That does not make quetiapine safer — its harms are documented at 25-100mg — but it does mean the two are not doing the same thing pharmacologically at their respective sleep doses. See [[wiki/concepts/drugs/Olanzapine#Does 5mg change the answer?|Olanzapine#Does 5mg change the answer?]].

The one genuinely favourable thing in the olanzapine file — that it deepens sleep rather than only sedating — does not transfer. Everything unfavourable does.

## It does not deepen sleep — it adds stage 2

*Good evidence, small study.* Cohrs et al. 2004 (*Psychopharmacology*; double-blind, placebo-controlled, randomised cross-over; n=14 healthy males; 25mg and 100mg one hour before bed; nights under standard and acoustic-stress conditions).

> Quetiapine 25 mg and 100 mg significantly improved sleep induction and continuity under standard and acoustic stress conditions. Increases in total sleep time, sleep efficiency, percentage sleep stage 2 and subjective sleep quality were seen.

> A significant increase in periodic leg movements during sleep was observed with quetiapine 100 mg.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15029469/

Stage 2 is light sleep. Slow-wave sleep and REM were essentially unchanged. This is the direct contrast with [[wiki/concepts/drugs/Olanzapine#Acute effect in healthy volunteers: large SWS increase|olanzapine's 59-83% SWS increase in the equivalent study]] — same design, same population type, similar size, opposite result on the measure that matters. More time asleep, not better sleep.

The periodic leg movements finding is worth noting: at 100mg, in healthy young men, in the same study that is used to argue quetiapine is good for sleep.

## The one RCT in primary insomnia was null

*No real evidence.* Tassniyom et al. 2010 (*J Med Assoc Thai*; randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled; quetiapine 25mg nightly for 2 weeks; primary insomnia by DSM-IV-TR; **13 patients completed**).

> Quetiapine group increased mean TST by 124.92 minutes and 72.24 minutes in the placebo group. Mean SL was reduced by 96.16 minutes in the Quetiapine group and 23.72 minutes in the placebo group. Statistical significance was not reached between both groups.

> Quetiapine at 25 mg at night did show a trend for improvement of TST and reduced SL in primary insomnia with few side effects but not reaching statistical significance.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20572379/

Note the placebo arm: **72 minutes more sleep and 24 minutes faster onset on nothing at all.** That is the size of the expectancy effect this drug is being prescribed against, and it is most of the drug's apparent benefit.

This trial is still, fifteen years on, the only randomised evidence in primary insomnia. An AHRQ review found nothing else meeting inclusion criteria.

> A 2012 summary of a 2011 Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality review...was inconclusive concerning the use of quetiapine for insomnia. Only 1 relevant study met the authors' inclusion criteria: a 2010 study in just 13 patients with primary insomnia.

https://www.ccjm.org/content/88/5/286

## In depression it did not even improve sleep continuity

*Weak evidence.* This is the second divergence from olanzapine. Milev et al. 2010 (*Neuropsychiatric Disease and Treatment*; prospective, **single-blind**, repeated measures, overnight polysomnography; adjunctive quetiapine, mean dose 155mg, range 100-200mg; 21-28 days; unipolar and bipolar depression).

> Adjunctive quetiapine therapy did not significantly alter sleep efficiency, sleep continuity, or Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index scores.

> Respiratory Disturbance Index and percentage of total time in rapid eye movement (REM) sleep significantly decreased and the percentage of total time in non-REM sleep, and duration of Stage 2 and non-REM sleep significantly increased after 2-4 days of quetiapine treatment.

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2938299/

Sleep continuity was the *one* thing olanzapine did improve in the equivalent population ([[wiki/concepts/drugs/Olanzapine#The placebo-controlled trial in patients missed its primary endpoint|Lazowski 2014]]). Quetiapine did not do it. Same research group, same city, comparable protocol — which makes the comparison unusually fair. Caveat: single-blind and uncontrolled, so weaker than the olanzapine trial it is being compared with.

## Guidelines name it directly

*Strong recommendation, weak evidence base.* Where the guidelines say "antipsychotics", the drug they have in mind is this one.

> weak recommendation against using antipsychotic drugs, including quetiapine, for the treatment of chronic insomnia disorder

— VA/DoD 2025

> does not recommend quetiapine and other antipsychotics for the treatment of insomnia

— European insomnia guideline 2023, rated A (very strong) despite no supporting evidence being identified

https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK619998/

## The scale of off-label use

This is the real difference from olanzapine: nobody much takes olanzapine for sleep, and enormous numbers of people take quetiapine for it.

British Columbia, 2010: about 1 million quetiapine prescriptions, the 9th most-prescribed drug in the province.

> 58% of B.C. quetiapine prescriptions were for the 25 mg tablet

— against a licensed antipsychotic range of 150-800mg/day. In the US, up to 70% of newer antipsychotic prescribing is for conditions other than psychosis.

https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK598436/

Canadian family-physician prescriptions rose 300% from 2005 to 2012; quetiapine ranks fourth among drugs prescribed for insomnia in the US (CCJM, above).

### AstraZeneca was fined specifically for marketing it for sleeplessness

*Strong evidence* — court record. #pharma

The user's note on [[wiki/concepts/sleep/Sleep#Antipsychotics - major tranquillisers - mood stabilisers|Sleep]] says the companies "have been fined billions for pushing off-label use to treat insomnia". For quetiapine that is exactly right, and more precisely so than for olanzapine — Eli Lilly's Zyprexa plea was about dementia, but AstraZeneca's Seroquel settlement names sleeplessness.

AstraZeneca paid $520m in April 2010 to settle civil claims that between January 2001 and December 2006 it promoted Seroquel for unapproved uses including sleeplessness, targeting doctors who do not treat schizophrenia or bipolar disorder — primary care physicians, geriatricians, paediatricians, long-term care facilities and prisons. At the time it was the largest civil-only off-label settlement the DoJ had obtained.

https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/pharmaceutical-giant-astrazeneca-pay-520-million-label-drug-marketing

Note this was a civil settlement without admission of liability, unlike Lilly's criminal guilty plea.

## Harms at sleep doses

"Low dose" does not mean low risk. The relevant harms are documented at 25-200mg, not just at antipsychotic doses.

### Weight and metabolic

*Good evidence, modest average effect, large individual variation.* Sonim et al. 2025 (*Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica*, systematic review and meta-analysis, 8 studies, n=3,085, doses under 200mg/day) found a mean weight gain of **0.58 kg (95% CI 0.32-0.83)** plus reduced HDL.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40772433/

That average conceals a lot. In a chart review of psychiatric patients on ≤200mg at bedtime for insomnia (n=43):

> daily dosages below 200 mg at bedtime...were associated with an average weight gain of 4.9 lb

and longitudinally, weight gain compounds rather than plateaus:

> 5.56 ± 1.25 lb at 6 months and 10.58 ± 2.20 lb at 12 months

Also reported: systolic BP +1.95 mmHg, diastolic +1.97 mmHg, BMI +0.52 kg/m², fasting glucose +6.71 mg/dL. https://www.ccjm.org/content/88/5/286

Less than olanzapine's ~11 kg, but not nothing, and paid nightly for a symptom.

### Older adults: mortality, dementia and falls

*Some evidence* — retrospective cohort, and the confounding is serious. Trask et al. 2025 (*Drugs & Aging*; retrospective cohort, US, patients 65+ starting **low-dose quetiapine specifically for insomnia** Oct 2018 - Sep 2021; n=375 quetiapine propensity-matched 4:1 to 1,500 on trazodone and 1,500 on mirtazapine).

> Comparing patients who received quetiapine with trazodone, the quetiapine group had an increased risk of mortality (HR 3.1, 95% CI 1.2-8.1), dementia (HR 8.1, 95% CI 4.1-15.8), and falls (HR 2.8, 95% CI 1.4-5.3). When comparing quetiapine with mirtazapine, quetiapine group had an increased risk of dementia (HR 7.1, 95% CI 3.5-14.4).

> Caution should be taken in practice when using low-dose quetiapine for insomnia in older adults.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39747780/

**Reverse causation is a live problem here.** A dementia hazard ratio of 8.1 is implausibly large for a drug effect over three years. The likelier reading is confounding by indication: an older patient with early behavioural disturbance, agitation or sleep-wake disruption is far more likely to be started on quetiapine than trazodone, and those are prodromal dementia symptoms. The mortality and falls findings are more believable, and the falls result has a clear mechanism (sedation plus orthostatic hypotension). *Likely:* the dementia signal is substantially inflated by indication bias; the drug is still the wrong choice in this group, but not because it causes dementia eightfold. This matters for the general claim on [[wiki/concepts/sleep/Sleep#Sleeping pills|Sleep#Sleeping pills]] that sleeping pills are linked to dementia — the association is real across drug classes, the causal reading is much weaker than the hazard ratios suggest.

Standard antipsychotic-class harms apply: black box warning for increased death in elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis. See [[wiki/concepts/drugs/Olanzapine#Elderly and dementia — black box|Olanzapine#Elderly and dementia]] for the parallel.

### Movement disorders and restless legs

*Some evidence.* Extrapyramidal symptoms in roughly 7-12% of patients in bipolar studies, including restless legs syndrome and akathisia (CCJM). The Therapeutics Initiative letter lists restless leg syndrome exacerbation and increased periodic leg movements among the harms, and Cohrs 2004 found PLMS at 100mg in healthy volunteers. As with olanzapine, this is the drug causing the complaint it is prescribed for, and it is often misread as agitation.

### Other

- **Orthostatic hypotension.** Two of fourteen healthy volunteers withdrew from Cohrs 2004 because of it.
- **QTc prolongation**, dose-dependent, carried on the label.
- **Sleep apnoea.** Largely unstudied, and the population overlaps heavily. Fauska et al. 2024 (*Journal of Sleep Research*, review) note that many people with insomnia have undiagnosed OSA and that quetiapine's effects on it are essentially unknown. Weight gain would be expected to worsen it. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37833613/
- **Abuse potential** is documented for quetiapine, unusually for an antipsychotic (Therapeutics Initiative).

## Where it is defensible

Same shape as the olanzapine answer. As a single drug doing two jobs when an antipsychotic or mood stabiliser is indicated anyway — schizophrenia, bipolar, treatment-resistant depression — with severe insomnia alongside. Some support exists for PTSD-related insomnia specifically. The CCJM bottom line:

> Evidence is insufficient to support the broad use of quetiapine to treat insomnia in the general patient population.

> Other FDA-approved medications for sleep should be tried before quetiapine.

Not as a sleeping pill in an otherwise well person, and CBT-i remains first line — see [[wiki/concepts/sleep/Sleep#CBT-i|Sleep#CBT-i]].

## Debunks

**It's not true that low-dose quetiapine is a "gentle" sleeping pill.** The 25mg dose is below the antipsychotic range but is not below the range at which weight gain, orthostatic hypotension, periodic leg movements and falls occur. The dose is low relative to schizophrenia treatment, not relative to the harms.

**It's not true that quetiapine preserves sleep architecture better than a Z-drug.** That claim is imported from the olanzapine literature. Quetiapine's own polysomnography shows extra stage 2 and unchanged slow-wave sleep — closer to a sedative profile than to anything restorative.
