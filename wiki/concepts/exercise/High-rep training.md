#exercise #gym

**Summary:** Research note on the user's plan: one or two all-out sets of 12-25 reps per exercise. For muscle size, light loads taken close to failure grow muscle about as well as heavy loads (meta-analysis, 21 studies), provided the load is above roughly 30-40% 1RM — 12-25 reps is comfortably inside that. Heavy loads win clearly for 1RM strength. Nobody has tested skipping the warm-up for light sets; the warm-up literature is about performance on heavy sets, and injury evidence for warm-ups is weak overall. No study compares injury rates by load; the indirect data say weight training injury rates are low across the board. Real downsides: less strength, more discomfort per set, failure becomes mandatory rather than optional, and tendons adapt less to light loads.

Related: [[Gym]] · [[Exercise]] · [[Protein]] · [[Creatine]]

# Does low load give the same muscle growth?

*Good evidence* for size: similar. *Strong evidence* that heavier loads give more 1RM strength.

Meta-analysis of 21 studies, all sets to failure, low load ≤60% 1RM vs high load >60% 1RM: hypertrophy equal, 1RM strength favours heavy.

> Gains in 1RM strength were significantly greater in favor of high- vs. low-load training, whereas no significant differences were found for isometric strength between conditions. Changes in measures of muscle hypertrophy were similar between conditions. The findings indicate that maximal strength benefits are obtained from the use of heavy loads while muscle hypertrophy can be equally achieved across a spectrum of loading ranges.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/ (Schoenfeld, Grgic, Ogborn, Krieger 2017, *J Strength Cond Res*)

Direct trial, 25 untrained men, 8 weeks, knee extension, volume-matched: 30% 1RM to failure (~34 reps) grew the quads as much as 80% 1RM (7.8% vs 8.1%), but 1RM strength gain was half (17.7% vs 33.8%).

> Quadriceps CSA increased significantly for HL-RF (8.1%, ES: 0.57), HL-RNF (7.7%, ES: 0.60), and LL-RF (7.8%, ES: 0.45), whereas no significant changes were observed in the LL-RNF (2.8%, ES: 0.15). We conclude that when training with low loads, training with a high level of effort seems to have greater importance than total training volume in the accretion of muscle mass, whereas for high load training, muscle failure does not promote any additional benefits.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31895290/ (Lasevicius et al. 2022, RCT within-subject, n=25)

## The floor is about 30% 1RM

Below ~30% 1RM growth drops off even when sets go to failure. 20% 1RM gave about half the hypertrophy of 40-80% in a 12-week volume-matched trial (n=30).

> G80 showed higher CSA than G20 for VL (19.5% vs. 8.9%) and EF (25% vs. 11.4%) at post-12 weeks (p < .05)... all intensities were effective for increasing muscle strength and size; however, 20% 1RM was suboptimal in this regard

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29564973/ (Lasevicius et al. 2018, *Eur J Sport Sci*)

Schoenfeld's 2021 re-examination of the repetition continuum puts the threshold at roughly 30% 1RM and confirms the "strength zone" for heavy loads.

> the minimum threshold is somewhere in the range of 30% 1RM

> load is the dominant variable for increasing 1RM, with other variables seemingly of secondary consequence

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7927075/ (Schoenfeld et al. 2021, narrative review)

Likely: 12-25 reps to failure sits around 50-70% 1RM on standard rep tables, so it is a "moderate-to-low" load well above the floor, not the 30% extreme the trials tested. The 30-rep-plus sets in the low-load studies are harder than what is proposed here.

# Close to failure is what makes low load work

*Good evidence.* At low load, stopping short of failure roughly wiped out the growth (2.8% vs 7.8% above). At high load it made no difference. So the plan's "full out" part is not optional.

Meta-regression across the failure literature: hypertrophy rises as sets get closer to failure; strength does not.

> Strength gains were similar across a wide range of RIR, while muscle hypertrophy improves as sets are terminated closer to failure.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/ (Robinson et al. 2024, *Sports Med*, meta-regression)

Categorical meta-analysis (15 studies) found no overall difference between failure and non-failure, with a small hypertrophy benefit of failure in trained people.

> Training to muscle failure does not seem to be required for gains in strength and muscle size. However, training in this manner does not seem to have detrimental effects on these adaptations, either.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33497853/ (Grgic et al. 2022, *J Sport Health Sci*)

# Can the warm-up be skipped for a light set?

*Weak evidence either way.* No study tests skipping warm-up before light, high-rep working sets. What exists:

- Warm-up research is about performance on heavy sets. In 40 trained men, a warm-up of a few reps at 40% of the training load was "not enough" to optimise 3×6 at 80% squat and bench performance; heavier warm-up sets did better.

> warming-up with few repetitions and low loads is not enough to optimize squat and bench press performances.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32971729/ (Ribeiro et al. 2020, crossover, n=40)

- Warm-up for injury prevention: five RCTs, three positive, two null, all in sport rather than lifting.

> There is insufficient evidence to endorse or discontinue routine warm-up prior to physical activity to prevent injury among sports participants. However, the weight of evidence is in favour of a decreased risk of injury.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16679062/ (Fradkin, Gabbe, Cameron 2006, systematic review of RCTs)

- Practitioner guidance (MacroFactor / Stronger by Science team, not a study): the lighter the working set, the less warm-up is needed; light or isolation movements may skip it.

> If your training session does not include heavier compound lifts, you can usually keep warm-ups simple and avoid overthinking load progression.

https://macrofactor.com/warm-up-resistance-training/

Likely: a 20-rep set warms itself up — the first reps at 55% 1RM are what a warm-up set would be anyway. The performance argument for warming up mostly vanishes at light loads. The injury argument was never strong. One light set of 8-10 on compound lifts (bench, row, shoulder press) is cheap insurance and the only place the user might keep it; isolation and cable work can go straight in. This is an inference from the above, not a tested result.

# Is it safer?

*No direct evidence.* No trial compares injury rates between light and heavy loading. Indirect data:

Injury rates are low across weight training generally, and lowest in bodybuilding, the discipline that trains most like this plan (moderate loads, higher reps).

> Bodybuilding had the lowest injury rates (0.12-0.7 injuries per lifter per year; 0.24-1 injury per 1000 h), with strongman (4.5-6.1 injuries per 1000 h) and Highland Games (7.5 injuries per 1000 h) reporting the highest rates... the weight-training sports appear to have relatively low rates of injury compared with common team sports.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27328853/ (Keogh & Winwood 2017, systematic review, mostly retrospective)

> This systematic review showed that traditional strength training is the safest RT method, and strongman is the least safe regarding injuries.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37046275/ (Serafim et al. 2023, systematic review, 28 studies)

Likely: lower peak joint and tendon forces per rep make acute injury less likely, which is why light loads are the rehab default. The offsetting risk is form breaking down in the last reps of an all-out 20-rep set, which no study has measured. Unverified either way.

# Downsides

1. **Less strength.** Roughly half the 1RM gain in the head-to-head trial above (17.7% vs 33.8%), and consistent across the meta-analysis. Isometric strength is unaffected. Matters if the goal includes lifting heavy things, less if the goal is size and health.

2. **Failure is compulsory, and failure has costs.** Low load only works at high effort (Lasevicius 2022). Sets to failure bring more fatigue, muscle damage and discomfort than stopping 1-2 reps short, with little extra growth at heavier loads.

> RT to set failure is likely not superior to non-failure RT for inducing muscle hypertrophy, but may exacerbate neuromuscular fatigue, muscle damage, and post-set perceived discomfort versus non-failure RT.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35658845/ (Refalo et al. 2022, scoping review)

3. **It hurts more per set.** Light-load sets to failure score higher on discomfort and perceived exertion, and lower on enjoyment, which is the adherence risk.

> training with low-loads tends to produce more discomfort, displeasure, and a higher rating of perceived exertion than training with moderate-to-high loads

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7927075/ (Schoenfeld et al. 2021)

Single-set lumbar extension, 6 weeks, 50% vs 80% MVC to failure: equal strength gains, discomfort 7.8 vs 4.8 out of 10.

> Mean repetitions per set, total training time and discomfort were all significantly higher for LL compared to HL (26 ± 8 vs. 8 ± 3 repetitions, 158.5 ± 47 vs. 50.5 ± 15 s, and 7.8 ± 1.8 vs. 4.8 ± 2.5, respectively; all p < 0.005).

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30498645/ (Fisher et al. 2018, RCT, n=26)

Failure vs 1-2 reps in reserve over 8 weeks in trained lifters: failure sets rated more discomfort (5.1 vs 4.1), higher RPE and worse post-session feeling.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39960821/ (Refalo et al. 2025, within-subject RCT, n=18)

4. **Tendons respond to load magnitude.** Meta-analysis of 37 interventions: tendon stiffness adapts to how heavy the load is, not to contraction type. Light-only training likely under-trains tendons, which matters for long-run resilience.

> the data strongly suggests that loading magnitude in particular plays a key role for tendon adaptation in contrast to muscle contraction type.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27747846/ (Bohm, Mersmann, Arampatzis 2015, meta-analysis)

5. **One or two sets is a minimum dose, not an optimum.** Single sets to failure do produce gains, but volume has a positive dose-response with diminishing returns.

> All identified studies showed that a single set performed minimum 1 time and maximum 3 times per week was sufficient to induce significant 1RM strength gains.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31797219/ (Androulakis-Korakakis, Fisher, Steele 2020, systematic review, trained men, 6 studies)

> gains in muscle size and strength increase as volume increases. However, both best-fit models suggest diminishing returns, with the diminishing returns for strength being considerably more pronounced.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/ (Pelland et al. 2026, *Sports Med*, meta-regression, 67 studies, n=2058)

6. **Very high reps stop being "lifting".** Above ~30-35 reps the set is limited by burn and breathing before the muscle is fully taxed; that is the mechanism behind the 20% 1RM floor. 12-25 reps stays clear of it.

# What this means for the plan

Sourced points restated, then the agent's read:

- Size: 1-2 sets of 12-25 to failure will grow muscle about as well per set as heavy sets. Sourced.
- Strength: expect noticeably smaller 1RM gains. Sourced.
- Warm-up: no evidence it is needed before light sets; one easy set on compound lifts is the cautious middle. Inference.
- Safety: probably lower acute risk, nothing measured. Inference.
- Likely the cheapest fix for the downsides is to keep one heavier exercise per session (e.g. bench or shoulder press at 6-10 reps) for strength and tendon loading, and take the rest to 12-25. Schoenfeld 2021 notes a possible benefit to combining loading ranges but calls the practical implications "questionable".

> acute and longitudinal data suggest a potential hypertrophic benefit to combining loading ranges as part of a structured RT program, although the practical implications of findings remain questionable

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7927075/

# Alternating heavy and light sessions

The user's plan (31 Aug 2026): same exercise heavy in A1, light in A2 (see [[Gym]]).

Heavy-day target: **6-10 reps at ~75-85% 1RM, 1-2 sets, stop 1-2 reps short of failure, after two ramp sets (e.g. 50% ×6, 75% ×3).** Basis:

- A single set of 6-12 reps at 70-85% 1RM, 2-3×/week, was enough for 1RM gains in trained men (Androulakis-Korakakis 2020, above).
- Failure adds nothing at 80% 1RM (Lasevicius 2022: 8.1% vs 7.7% growth, 33.8% vs 33.4% strength) but adds fatigue and discomfort (Refalo 2022, 2025).
- A light warm-up was not enough to optimise 80% sets (Ribeiro 2020), so the ramp sets belong on the heavy day, not the light one.
- Combining loading ranges may help hypertrophy; Schoenfeld 2021 calls the practical implications "questionable", i.e. not shown to be worse.

Likely: keep heavy loading to lifts that take it well (bench, seated/cable row, shoulder press) and leave lunges, pull-throughs, dips, curls and triceps work at 10-15 either day. Inference, not a tested result.

## Isolation exercises can stay light

*Good evidence.* The load-independence trials above were mostly single-joint: knee extension (Lasevicius 2022), elbow flexion (Lasevicius 2018), lumbar extension (Fisher 2018). The reasons to go heavy — 1RM strength and tendon stiffness — matter little for curls, extensions and cable work, and the compound lifts on the heavy day already load the main tendons. So isolation and cable work stays at 12-25 to failure every session, no ramp sets. Unverified: the common practitioner claim that heavy curls/skullcrushers are where elbow tendon complaints start.

# One myo-rep set vs two standard sets

*Some evidence* — one direct trial plus the rest-pause and drop-set literature: equal growth in less time, at a higher perceived cost.

Myo-reps (Fagerli): an activation set to ~1 rep in reserve, then 3-5 breaths' rest and mini-sets of 3-5 reps, repeated until the mini-set count drops. The only direct trial (22 trained men, 8 weeks, bench/incline/cable fly) found equal size and strength against traditional straight sets with ~30% less volume-load.

> There were no differences between MYO and TRA on measures of muscle excitation, mean barbell velocity, muscle size, and strength. Despite there being no differences between groups, MYO can still be a viable option to achieve similar results in less time.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42112925/ (Bradshaw et al. 2026, *J Strength Cond Res*, n=22)

Rest-pause, volume-equated, 28 trained men, 8 weeks: hypertrophy equal, squat 1RM slightly better.

> Our findings suggest that RP promotes slightly superior strength-related improvements compared with TRT, but hypertrophic adaptations are similar between conditions.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34260860/ (Enes et al. 2021, RCT)

Earlier 6-week trial (n=18) had rest-pause ahead on thigh thickness (11% vs 1%) with equal strength — small, noisy, favourable direction.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28617715/ (Prestes et al. 2019, RCT)

Drop sets, meta-analysis of 12 studies (n=274): same growth and strength, much higher perceived exertion.

> No significant differences were observed between DROP and TRAD for chronic hypertrophy (SMD = 0.04, 95% CI [- 0.29 to 0.36]), strength (SMD = - 0.04, 95% CI [- 0.34 to 0.26])... DROP acutely induces significantly higher perceived exertion and lactate responses

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41920484/ (Havers et al. 2026, meta-analysis)

The cost: rest-pause sessions rated RPE 8.5 vs 6.0 and pain 8 vs 5 out of 10 against traditional sets, and post-session affect went from positive to neutral-or-negative.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39534528/ (Almeida et al. 2024, acute crossover, n=15)

Likely: on the light day and for isolation work, one myo-rep set can replace two straight sets. Keep straight sets on the heavy day, since myo-reps rely on working near failure and the heavy set should not. Inference from the above.

# Mechanisms

Low load recruits high-threshold motor units only as fatigue accumulates near the end of the set, which is why proximity to failure matters more at light loads than heavy (Schoenfeld 2021, Robinson 2024 above). Heavy loads recruit them from the first rep.
