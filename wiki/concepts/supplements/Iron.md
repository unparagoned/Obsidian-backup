#iron #supplements

**Summary:** Iron is needed for haemoglobin and oxygen transport; deficiency (with or without anaemia) is common in menstruating women and causes fatigue. Supplementation helps only when iron is low: it reduces subjective fatigue in non-anaemic iron-deficient adults and treats restless legs when ferritin is low, but does nothing for people who are replete and is toxic in excess or in haemochromatosis.
**Evidence:** Strong evidence (anaemia, iron-deficiency); Good evidence, modest effect (fatigue in non-anaemic iron deficiency, ferritin under ~50 µg/L); Some evidence (restless legs with ferritin ≤75 µg/L); No real evidence (exercise capacity when non-anaemic). Dose: RDA 8 mg (men, post-menopausal women) / 18 mg (pre-menopausal women); UL 45 mg/day; treatment 65-80 mg elemental iron/day, test ferritin first.
Related: [[Supplements]], [[Sleep]], [[Exercise]], [[Depression]], [[D vitamin]], [[Magnesium]], [[Creatine]]

## Fatigue in non-anaemic women
Double-blind RCT (BMJ 2003, n=144, 80 mg elemental iron/day for 4 weeks): fatigue fell more on iron; benefit confined to ferritin ≤50 µg/L.
> The level of fatigue after one month decreased by -1.82/6.37 points (29%) in the iron group compared with -0.85/6.46 points (13%) in the placebo group (difference 0.95 points, 95% confidence interval 0.32 to 1.62; P=0.004). Subgroups analysis showed that only women with ferritin concentrations <or= 50 microg/l improved with oral supplementation.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12763985/

Systematic review of 18 RCTs (n=1170) in iron-deficient non-anaemic adults: less self-reported fatigue, no change in objective exercise capacity.
> iron supplementation was associated with reduced self-reported fatigue (standardised mean difference (SMD) -0.38; 95% CI -0.52 to -0.23; I2 0%; 4 trials; 714 participants) but was not associated with differences in objective measures of physical capacity, including maximal oxygen consumption (SMD 0.11; 95% CI -0.15 to 0.37; I2 0%; 9 trials; 235 participants) and timed methods of exercise testing.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29626044/

## Restless legs and sleep #sleep
IRLSSG guideline (2018): IV ferric carboxymaltose is first-line for moderate-severe RLS with ferritin <300 µg/L; oral iron possibly effective if ferritin ≤75 µg/L.
> Ferric carboxymaltose (1000 mg) is effective for treating moderate to severe RLS in those with serum ferritin <300 μg/l and could be used as first-line treatment for RLS in adults. Oral iron (65 mg elemental iron) is possibly effective for treating RLS in those with serum ferritin ≤75 μg/l.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29425576/

## Anaemia
Cochrane overview of 75 systematic reviews (2021): daily iron raises haemoglobin and cuts anaemia across age groups.
> Compared to no treatment, daily iron supplementation may increase Hb levels and reduce the risk of anaemia and IDA in infants, preschool and school-aged children and pregnant and non-pregnant women.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34564844/

## Exercise #exercise
See the 18-trial review above: subjective fatigue improved, VO2max did not. Unverified: athletes (especially endurance and female) have higher deficiency rates; the NIH ODS athletic-performance sheet could not be fetched.

## Dose
> The recommended dietary allowance (RDA) for iron is 8 mg/day for men and postmenopausal women, 18 mg/day for premenopausal women, and 27 mg/day for pregnant women.
> The UL for adolescents (14-18 years) and adults, including pregnant and breastfeeding women, is 45 mg/day.
https://lpi.oregonstate.edu/mic/minerals/iron

## Safety and interactions
GI side effects are common; iron blocks absorption of several drugs; coffee and tea block non-haem iron absorption.
> At therapeutic levels used to treat iron deficiency, iron supplements may cause gastrointestinal irritation, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, or constipation.
> Taking iron supplements at the same time as the following medications may result in decreased absorption and efficacy of the medication: carbidopa and levodopa (Sinemet), levothyroxine (Synthroid, Levoxyl), methyldopa (Aldomet), penicillamine (Cuprimine, Depen), quinolones, tetracyclines, and bisphosphonates (120).
> Polyphenolic compounds in coffee, black tea, and herbal tea can markedly inhibit the absorption of nonheme iron (76).
https://lpi.oregonstate.edu/mic/minerals/iron

Haemochromatosis: iron loading damages organs; supplementing without testing is the risk.
> Toxic iron deposition in vital organs in patients affected by hereditary hemochromatosis has been associated with numerous chronic conditions, including liver cancer and type 2 diabetes mellitus.
https://lpi.oregonstate.edu/mic/minerals/iron

## Conflicting evidence
Fatigue vs capacity: trials show reduced self-reported fatigue but no objective performance gain in non-anaemic deficiency (Houston 2018 above). Whether the fatigue effect is real or expectation is not settled; the BMJ 2003 RCT was double-blind, which argues against pure placebo.

## Mechanisms
The body limits iron during infection; hepcidin is the control hormone.
> Iron is required by most infectious agents to grow and spread, as well as by the infected host in order to mount an effective immune response.
https://lpi.oregonstate.edu/mic/minerals/iron
