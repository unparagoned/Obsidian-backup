#potassium #supplements

**Summary:** Potassium is the main intracellular cation; higher intake lowers blood pressure in hypertensives and is associated with fewer strokes. The benefit is best obtained from food; the blood-pressure effect is non-linear and excessive supplementation can raise BP or cause hyperkalaemia in people with kidney impairment or on potassium-sparing drugs.
**Evidence:** Strong evidence (BP reduction in hypertension); Good evidence, modest effect (stroke, cohort data only); No real evidence found for sleep, depression, testosterone, exercise or dementia. Dose: AI 3,400 mg/day (men) / 2,600 mg/day (women); no UL set; trial benefit at 90-120 mmol/day (3.5-4.7 g).
Related: [[Supplements]], [[Magnesium]], [[Exercise]], [[Sleep]], [[Depression]]

## Blood pressure
WHO-commissioned systematic review (BMJ 2013): 22 RCTs (n=1606) and 11 cohorts (n=127,038). BP fell in hypertensives only; no harm to kidney function or lipids.
> Increased potassium intake reduced systolic blood pressure by 3.49 (95% confidence interval 1.82 to 5.15) mm Hg and diastolic blood pressure by 1.96 (0.86 to 3.06) mm Hg in adults, an effect seen in people with hypertension but not in those without hypertension. Systolic blood pressure was reduced by 7.16 (1.91 to 12.41) mm Hg when the higher potassium intake was 90-120 mmol/day, without any dose response.
> High quality evidence shows that increased potassium intake reduces blood pressure in people with hypertension and has no adverse effect on blood lipid concentrations, catecholamine concentrations, or renal function in adults.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23558164/

Dose-response meta-analysis of 32 RCTs (JAHA 2020): U-shaped relation; more is not better, especially in treated hypertensives.
> The BP-lowering effects of potassium supplementation were stronger in participants with hypertension and at higher levels of sodium intake.
> We identified a nonlinear relationship between potassium intake and both systolic and diastolic BP [...] adequate intake of potassium is desirable to achieve a lower BP level but suggest excessive potassium supplementation should be avoided, particularly in specific subgroups.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7429027/

## Stroke and cardiovascular disease
Meta-analysis of 11 prospective cohorts (n=247,510): higher intake associated with fewer strokes. Observational; residual confounding possible (high-potassium diets are fruit/vegetable-rich).
> In the pooled analysis, a 1.64-g (42 mmol) per day higher potassium intake was associated with a 21% lower risk of stroke (RR: 0.79; 95% CI: 0.68 to 0.90; p = 0.0007), with a trend toward lower risk of CHD and total CVD
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21371638/

The BMJ review's cohort arm found a similar figure.
> An inverse statistically significant association was seen between potassium intake and risk of incident stroke (risk ratio 0.76, 0.66 to 0.89).
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23558164/

## Kidney stones and bone
Linus Pauling Institute review: observational link to fewer kidney stones; one 2-year RCT of potassium citrate improved bone density.
> Results from observational studies reported higher dietary potassium intakes to be associated with lower risks of stroke and kidney stone formation.
> A two-year randomized, double-blind, controlled trial in 201 older adults without osteoporosis (mean age, 69 years) found evidence of increased lumbar spine, hip, femoral neck, and total-body BMD, as well as trabecular BMD of the radius and tibia, with supplemental potassium citrate (2,340 mg/day) compared to placebo
https://lpi.oregonstate.edu/mic/minerals/potassium

## Dose
Adequate intake, not RDA, because the data were insufficient.
> The adequate intake (AI) for potassium is 2,600 mg/day for women and 3,400 mg/day for men.
> The FNB did not find sufficient evidence to determine an estimated average requirement (EAR) and derive a recommended dietary allowance (RDA); instead, they established an adequate intake (AI) based on median intakes in generally healthy people
https://lpi.oregonstate.edu/mic/minerals/potassium

Unverified: US over-the-counter potassium supplements are typically capped at 99 mg per tablet; the source page could not be fetched.

## Safety and interactions
Hyperkalaemia occurs when intake exceeds renal excretion; kidney disease and potassium-sparing diuretics are the main risks.
> Hyperkalemia occurs when potassium intake exceeds the capacity of the kidneys to eliminate it.
> Acute or chronic kidney failure, the use of potassium-sparing diuretics, and insufficient aldosterone secretion (hypoaldosteronism) may result in the accumulation of potassium due to a decreased urinary potassium excretion.
> Individuals with abnormal kidney function and those on potassium-sparing medications (see Drug interactions) should be monitored closely to prevent hyperkalemia (50, 53).
https://lpi.oregonstate.edu/mic/minerals/potassium

Unverified: ACE inhibitors and ARBs also raise hyperkalaemia risk; not captured from a fetched source.

## Conflicting evidence
The BMJ 2013 review reports BP reduction "without any dose response" up to 90-120 mmol/day; the JAHA 2020 dose-response analysis finds a U-shaped curve with BP rising again at high supplemental doses in drug-treated hypertensives. Quotes above.

## Mechanisms
> Potassium (K+) is the principal positively charged ion (cation) in the fluid inside of cells, while sodium (Na+) is the principal cation in the extracellular fluid.
https://lpi.oregonstate.edu/mic/minerals/potassium
