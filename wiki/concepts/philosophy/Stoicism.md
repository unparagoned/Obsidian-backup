# Core idea

Stoicism is a practice, not a doctrine. Ethics is the point; logic and physics support it. Progress shows in behaviour.

>Another sign of progress in our practice of Stoicism is that our philosophy will consist of actions rather than words.

What hurts is the judgement, not the event.

>as Epictetus puts it, "what upsets people is not things themselves but their judgments about these things."

Thoughts shape happiness.

>"The happiness of your life depends on the quality of your thoughts." — Marcus Aurelius

>"It's not what happens to you, it's how you react that matters." — Epictetus

Attribution note: both are popular wordings, not direct translations. The Marcus line is a loose rendering of *Meditations* 5.16 ("the soul is dyed by the thoughts"); the Epictetus line is a paraphrase of *Enchiridion* 5, the same idea as the judgement quote above. #quotes

Irvine frames Stoicism as a set of techniques for overriding evolved emotional defaults with reason.

>Stoicism, understood properly, is a cure for a disease. The disease in question is the anxiety, grief, fear, and various other negative emotions that plague humans and prevent them from experiencing a joyful existence.

Full section: [[A guide to the good life#Stoic philosophy]].

# The five main Stoics

Zeno (founder, c. 300 BCE, Athens) → Musonius Rufus (teacher) → Seneca (adviser to Nero, wrote the letters) → Epictetus (born a slave; control what you can) → Marcus Aurelius (emperor, wrote *Meditations*). Memory chain: Founder → Mentor → Adviser → Slave → Emperor. Hooks and one-line bios: [[A guide to the good life#People]].

Correction: the book note says Musonius "taught Epicurus". He taught **Epictetus**; Epicurus founded a rival school three centuries earlier.


Zeno — the Founder. A merchant from Cyprus who ended up in Athens and founded Stoicism around 300 BCE. Memory hook: Zeno = Zero, the starting point.

Seneca — the Adviser. Wealthy Roman statesman, writer and adviser to Emperor Nero. He wrote practical letters about anger, death, time and adversity. Memory hook: Seneca = Senator. Think powerful political insider trying to live wisely in a corrupt world.

Gaius Musonius Rufus - teacher, taught Epicurus. Memory hook: Musonius The mentor

Epictetus — the Slave. Born enslaved, later freed, and became a philosophy teacher. His core idea: separate what you control from what you don’t. Memory hook: Epictetus = the ex-slave who mastered himself because he couldn’t control his circumstances.

Marcus Aurelius — the Emperor. Roman emperor and author of Meditations, which was essentially his private notebook reminding himself how to behave properly under pressure. Memory hook: Marcus = the man with maximum power reminding himself not to be an arsehole.

# Practices

## Negative visualisation

Imagine losing what you have. It counters hedonic adaptation and makes ordinary things feel like enough. Retrospective form: imagine never having had what you have lost, so a loss reads as gratitude for having had it.

>Because of adaptation, we take our life and what we have for granted rather than delighting in them. Negative visualization, though, is a powerful antidote to hedonic adaptation.

Full quotes: [[A guide to the good life#Negative visualisation]].

## Control and goals

Want only what is in your power. Set the goal at the effort, not the outcome: play your best, not win.

>Your primary desire, says Epictetus, should be your desire not to be frustrated by forming desires you won't be able to fulfill.

You are an actor in a play written by the Fates; you don't choose the role, you play it well. Unhappiness is your own responsibility, not the government's. Full quotes: [[A guide to the good life#Personal responsibility and self control]].

## Hardship as training

>God, he said, "does not make a spoiled pet of a good man; he tests him, hardens him, and fits him for his own service."

Seneca, via [[A guide to the good life#Hardship]].

Obstacles fuel progress.

>"The impediment to action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way." — Marcus Aurelius

*Meditations* 5.20 (Hays translation). #quotes

## Dealing with people

Other people are the main threat to tranquility. Marcus Aurelius's method, as summarised in the user's note: expect bad behaviour so it isn't a surprise; treat it as ignorance, so it's unintentional and not blameworthy; if they don't deserve power over you, don't be moved by them; self-control.

>BEGIN EACH DAY by telling yourself: Today I shall be meeting with interference, ingratitude, insolence, disloyalty, ill-will, and selfishness—all of them due to the offenders' ignorance of what is good or evil.
>Marcus Aurelius

Full section: [[A guide to the good life#Socialising]].

## Don't suffer in advance

Avoid suffering in advance. Worry is paying for a loss that may never come; negative visualisation is a deliberate exercise, not a habit of dread.

>"He who suffers before it is necessary suffers more than is necessary." — Seneca

*Letters to Lucilius* 98. #quotes

## Ego blocks learning

>"It's impossible to learn that which you think you already know." — Epictetus

*Discourses* 2.17. #quotes

## Minimalism

Train yourself to be satisfied with what you have rather than working to become wealthy; overcome the craving for admiration rather than chasing fame; keep true friends rather than popularity (Musonius). [[A guide to the good life#Minimalism]].
