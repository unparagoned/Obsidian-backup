## Summary

Two questions. First, whether [[Michael Levin]] says slime mould is intelligent — answered yes, on a broad functional definition of intelligence that does not require neurons. Second, whether someone debunked it as "just physics" and Levin disagreed — answered yes, with the debunk attributed to Andrew Adamatzky and Levin's reply summarised as "brains also operate through physics".

The four cited links are real and say roughly what the conversation claims. The framing of the second answer is the part that doesn't survive checking; see **Where the source is misleading** below.

## Key claims, checked

- **Levin does treat slime mould as cognitive.** Confirmed in Harvard Magazine, Nancy Walecki, "Can Slime Molds Think?", 6 October 2021.

> Cognition is all around you. It really is everywhere...not just for furry, brainy things.

https://www.harvardmagazine.com/2021/10/right-now-can-slime-molds-think

- **He co-authored a paper on slime mould's minimal cognition.** Confirmed: Vallverdú, Castro, Mayne, Talanov, **Levin**, Baluška, Gunji, Dussutour, Zenil and **Adamatzky**, "Slime mould: The fundamental mechanisms of biological cognition", *BioSystems* 165 (2018) 57–70. Review/position paper, not an experiment. It sets out to "rehabilitate" *Physarum* "from the rank of a purely living electronics element to a 'creature of thoughts'".

https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0303264717304574

- **The maze-solving debunk is real.** Andrew Adamatzky, "Maze solvers demystified and some other thoughts", arXiv 2017.

> We show that most prototypes utilise one of two phenomena: a shortest path in a maze is a path of the least resistance for fluid and current flow, and a shortest path is a path of the steepest gradient of chemoattractants. We discuss that substrates with so-called maze-solving capabilities simply trace flow currents or chemical diffusion gradients.

https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.04681

- **Levin's reply is real and is on the record.** thoughtforms.life Q&A, 29 August 2025, answering a video that argued water solves mazes too.

> Right, aspects of physics and materials properties underlie intelligence (as is well-understood and exploited by the field of morphological computing and others). Is the claim in that video that "real intelligence" has to mean there's no physics underneath?

> The confusion in the argument in that video is caused by assuming a binary "is/is-not intelligent."

https://thoughtforms.life/qa-from-the-internet-and-recent-presentations-3/

## Where the source is misleading

- **Adamatzky is not Levin's opponent here — he is his co-author.** The conversation presents Adamatzky as the critic who "formalized essentially the same criticism" against Levin. Adamatzky is in fact a co-author of the very minimal-cognition paper cited as evidence *for* Levin's position, and is the leading figure in *Physarum* unconventional computing. The two positions — "maze-solving is gradient-following" and "slime mould has minimal cognition" — are held by the same person, in the same period. So this is not a debunker-versus-defender dispute, and treating it as one is the source's error, not the literature's.
- **The 2017 paper and the 2025 reply are not a direct exchange.** Adamatzky's paper predates Levin's post by eight years, and Levin's post is answering "that video", not Adamatzky. *Likely:* the video is "Hot Take: Slime mold is not intelligent" (YouTube, 4 August 2025), which fits the date and the argument — **unverified**, Levin's post does not name it.
- **The closing paragraphs are ChatGPT's own editorialising**, not anyone's published view: that Levin's definition "risks making 'intelligence' too inclusive to be informative" and that it "can become a redefinition rather than a scientific discovery". Reasonable, but unattributed — it is not sourced criticism.
- **Unverified in the source:** the claim that Levin "assigns even water a tiny non-zero degree of 'competence'". The fetched excerpt of the Q&A has him talking about competencies of material components generally, not water specifically.
