- **Query + research** "is olanzapine good for sleep" → new `## Sleep and insomnia` section on [[wiki/concepts/drugs/Olanzapine|Olanzapine]]; agent-researched material appended to [[wiki/concepts/sleep/Sleep#Antipsychotics - major tranquillisers - mood stabilisers|Sleep#Antipsychotics]] under the user's own paragraph (their prose untouched); `## Use for insomnia` added to [[Antipsychotics]]; index line updated.
  - **Answer**: no for ordinary insomnia. *No real evidence* — there is no randomised placebo-controlled trial of olanzapine in primary insomnia at all, and both the VA/DoD 2025 and European 2023 insomnia guidelines recommend against antipsychotics.
  - **The pro-sleep evidence is one 9-person study.** Sharpley et al. 2000 (*Biological Psychiatry*, placebo-controlled double-blind cross-over, healthy males, single 5/10mg evening doses) found 59.1% and 83.3% dose-related increases in slow-wave sleep. Almost every "olanzapine deepens sleep" claim traces to it.
  - **It did not replicate in patients.** Lazowski et al. 2014 (*BMC Psychiatry*, n=25, depression, 28-31 days, mean 6.67mg, home PSG) had SWS as its pre-specified primary outcome and was null (SWS 10% → 10% of TST); sleep continuity (secondary) improved — TST 326 → 363 min, efficiency 71% → 83%. Trial stopped early for failure to recruit; near-universal polypharmacy; 3/15 olanzapine patients withdrew with worsening mood; olanzapine group gained ~2 kg in the four weeks.
  - **Correction recorded, contradicting a source**: Sleep Science 2021 (Kermanshah, n=40, one year, no placebo) concludes olanzapine and quetiapine "have long-term efficacy in managing chronic insomnia". Its own actigraphy improved only in the quetiapine arm; for olanzapine only the self-report PSQI moved. Recorded as overstated on the page.
  - **Harm the user's note did not have**: olanzapine can cause restless legs syndrome and periodic limb movements, i.e. the sleep problem it is given for (Kang et al. 2009, *J Psychopharmacol* — five cases, case series only).
  - **User's note graded**: their existing paragraph ("will knock you out, maybe not in the right way... doctors probably shouldn't be prescribing these for insomnia") is supported on the conclusion. One qualification added — on acute architecture olanzapine looks *better* than Z-drugs and antihistamines, not worse; the reason to avoid it is metabolic and mortality cost, not sleep quality. Also noted that the Lilly guilty plea they refer to was for off-label promotion in **dementia**, not insomnia.
  - *Unverified:* the AASM 2017 pharmacologic insomnia guideline was not read directly (PDF would not extract). The VA/DoD 2025 and European 2023 quotes come from an NCBI Bookshelf review of quetiapine, not from the guideline documents themselves.
  - **Open**: no data found on tolerance to the sedative effect or rebound insomnia on stopping; no head-to-head against a DORA, trazodone or CBT-i. Logged on the page TODO.

- **Query + research** "would most of that follow over to Seroquel" (follow-up to the olanzapine sleep query) → created [[wiki/concepts/drugs/Quetiapine|Quetiapine]] in `wiki/concepts/drugs/`, closing the TODO opened on [[Antipsychotics]] the same day. Cross-linked from [[Antipsychotics]] (`## Use for insomnia` rewritten), [[wiki/concepts/drugs/Olanzapine|Olanzapine]] and [[wiki/concepts/sleep/Sleep#Antipsychotics - major tranquillisers - mood stabilisers|Sleep#Antipsychotics]]; index updated. No `raw/` source — agent research from primary literature.
  - **Answer**: mostly yes, and the two points where it diverges both make quetiapine's case worse, not better.
  - **Divergence 1, sleep architecture.** Olanzapine's one favourable finding does not transfer. Cohrs et al. 2004 (*Psychopharmacology*, double-blind placebo-controlled cross-over, n=14 healthy males, 25mg and 100mg) found quetiapine increased total sleep time, efficiency and **stage 2** — slow-wave and REM sleep essentially unchanged. Same design and population as the olanzapine study that showed a 59-83% SWS increase, opposite result. More sleep, not deeper sleep.
  - **Divergence 2, it has been tested and failed.** Tassniyom et al. 2010 (*J Med Assoc Thai*, randomised double-blind placebo-controlled, 25mg nightly, 2 weeks, **13 completers**) was null on every primary outcome. The placebo arm gained 72 min of total sleep time and 24 min of sleep latency unaided — most of the drug's apparent benefit. Still the only RCT in primary insomnia; a 2011 AHRQ review found nothing else meeting inclusion.
  - **Third difference**: quetiapine did *not* improve sleep continuity in depression (Milev et al. 2010, single-blind PSG, mean 155mg), which is the one thing olanzapine did improve in the comparable trial from the same group.
  - **What carries over unchanged**: guidelines against (and they name quetiapine explicitly), elderly black box, RLS/PLMS as a drug effect, next-day sedation, weight and metabolic harm.
  - **Correction to the user's note, in their favour**: their line that the companies were "fined billions for pushing off-label use to treat insomnia" is precisely right for Seroquel and not for Zyprexa. AstraZeneca's $520m civil settlement (April 2010) names sleeplessness and the targeting of primary care, geriatric, paediatric, long-term care and prison prescribers; Lilly's criminal plea was about dementia. Both now recorded on [[wiki/concepts/sleep/Sleep#Antipsychotics - major tranquillisers - mood stabilisers|Sleep]].
  - **Reverse causation flagged**: Trask et al. 2025 (*Drugs & Aging*, retrospective cohort, n=375 on low-dose quetiapine for insomnia, 65+, matched 4:1) reports mortality HR 3.1, dementia HR 8.1, falls HR 2.8 vs trazodone. Recorded with an explicit *Likely:* note that HR 8.1 for dementia over three years is implausible as a drug effect and is probably confounding by indication — quetiapine gets started for prodromal agitation and sleep-wake disruption. Falls and mortality are more credible. This also weakens the general "sleeping pills cause dementia" reading on [[wiki/concepts/sleep/Sleep#Sleeping pills|Sleep#Sleeping pills]].
  - **Scale**: 58% of ~1 million annual BC quetiapine prescriptions were 25mg tablets against a licensed 150-800mg/day range; Canadian family-physician prescribing rose 300% from 2005 to 2012; 4th most prescribed drug for insomnia in the US. Olanzapine is barely used this way — quetiapine is the actual clinical problem.
  - **Weight at sleep doses**: meta-analysis (Sonim et al. 2025, *Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica*, 8 studies, n=3,085, <200mg/day) gives +0.58 kg mean, but chart-review data show 5.56 lb at 6 months and 10.58 lb at 12 months — compounding, not plateauing.
  - **Two debunks recorded**: that low-dose quetiapine is a "gentle" sleeping pill (the dose is low relative to schizophrenia treatment, not relative to the harms), and that it preserves sleep architecture better than a Z-drug (that claim is imported from the olanzapine literature and quetiapine's own PSG does not support it).
  - *Unverified:* the "some patients gain 30 lb on 50mg" individual-variation claim came from a secondary source, not the meta-analysis; Milev 2010's sample size was not captured; the PTSD-insomnia evidence asserted by CCJM was not followed up. All on the page TODO.

- **Query + research** "what about doses of 5mg of olanzapine, is that still bad" → new `### Does 5mg change the answer?` subsection on [[wiki/concepts/drugs/Olanzapine#Does 5mg change the answer?|Olanzapine]]; weight-gain bullet corrected from "Dose-dependent" to "Only weakly dose-dependent"; a dose row added to the comparison table on [[wiki/concepts/drugs/Quetiapine|Quetiapine]].
  - **Answer**: better than 10-20mg, not a reprieve. The weight penalty falls by roughly a quarter to a third, not by most, and every non-metabolic objection is unchanged.
  - **Key correction to this vault's own page**: the weight section previously said "Dose-dependent". That overstates it. Schoretsanitis et al. 2022 (*Basic Clin Pharmacol Toxicol*, prospective cohort, n=392, median 10mg/day) found **weight gain not associated with dose (p=0.61)**; ≤10mg still gave 1.61% vs 2.54% above 10mg. Their conclusion: "olanzapine-treated patients are at weight gain risk regardless of the dose."
  - **5mg is not a low dose.** This is the main asymmetry with the quetiapine answer given earlier. Kapur et al. 1998 (*AJP*, PET, n=12 randomised to 5/10/15/20mg): 5-HT2 near-saturated **even at 5mg**, mean D2 occupancy ~60% against a ~65% therapeutic threshold. Olanzapine 5mg is the licensed starting dose; quetiapine 25mg is a sixth of the minimum antipsychotic dose and gives negligible D2 occupancy. In a 9,565-patient registry the "low-dose" cutoff was **<2.5mg** — 5mg counted as regular dose.
  - **Curve shape**: Sabé et al. 2022 (*Schizophrenia Bulletin*, dose-response meta-analysis, 97 studies, 36,326 participants; olanzapine 16 studies n=3,575, 1-40mg) found olanzapine among the minority whose weight curve never plateaus (40mg → MD 3.62 kg at 6 weeks), but "the slope of the curve was smaller beyond 10 mg/d". Read with the cohort: steep climb from zero then flattening, so the first 5mg buys most of the penalty.
  - **Awkward finding recorded rather than buried**: in the same cohort, *lower* doses were associated with increases in total and HDL cholesterol and systolic/diastolic BP, while higher doses drove glucose. Marked *Likely:* confounded by who gets low doses, not a genuine inverse effect.
  - **Point in favour of 5mg on the sleep side**: in Sharpley 2000, 5mg gave a 59.1% SWS increase against 83.3% at 10mg — most of the effect — and REM suppression appeared **only at 10mg**. So 5mg is both the safer and the better sleep dose.
  - **Very low dose may differ**: Korean registry (*J Personalized Medicine* 2025, n=9,565, 1,629 on <2.5mg) gives diabetes HR 0.32 and dyslipidaemia HR 0.59 vs regular dose. Flagged as *Likely confounded* — retrospective, no described adjustment for indication — and it is a quarter of 5mg, with no evidence it does anything for sleep.
  - *Unverified:* no extractable mean weight difference for 5mg specifically from the Sabé figure, so no number was invented. Nothing found on whether 2.5mg retains a hypnotic effect; Sharpley went no lower than 5mg. Both on the page TODO.
