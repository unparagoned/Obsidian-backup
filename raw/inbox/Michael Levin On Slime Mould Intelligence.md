Yes. Michael Levin explicitly treats slime mould—especially *Physarum polycephalum* —as displaying a basic form of intelligence or “basal cognition.”

His definition is broad and functional: intelligence is the capacity to sense conditions, retain information, make choices, and pursue goals through different means. *Physarum* can navigate environments, adapt its behaviour from experience, and choose among alternatives despite having no brain or nervous system. Levin describes it as having “a different form of intelligence” and says it can map its surroundings and make decisions. [^1] He also co-authored a paper specifically analyzing slime mould’s capacity for minimal cognition. [^2]

The important distinction: he is **not necessarily claiming that slime mould thinks or experiences consciousness like a human**. He is arguing that intelligence comes in degrees and does not require neurons. Whether we label that behaviour “intelligence,” “biological computation,” or sophisticated biochemical control depends heavily on how narrowly we define the word.

Yes. You’re remembering the dispute correctly.

A critic demonstrated that water—or another passive physical system—can “solve” certain mazes by following pressure, resistance, or chemical gradients. Andrew Adamatzky formalized essentially the same criticism: many celebrated maze-solving systems simply trace the path of least resistance or the steepest chemical gradient. [^3] That undermines the claim that **maze solving by itself proves intelligence**.

Levin’s response was basically:

> Discovering the physical mechanism doesn’t make the behaviour “not intelligence,” because brains also operate through physics.

He rejects intelligence as a yes/no category. He puts water, slime mould, cells, animals, and humans on a spectrum of problem-solving competence, with slime mould capable of more adaptive behaviour than water. He explicitly answered the “even water solves mazes” objection in 2025. [^4]

The honest assessment:

- The critic successfully debunked **the maze test as sufficient evidence of intelligence**.
- They did **not prove slime mould has zero cognition**; other evidence concerns habituation, memory-like effects, choice and adaptive behaviour.
- Levin’s answer is logically defensible, but his definition is unusually broad. He assigns even water a tiny non-zero degree of “competence.”
- That risks making “intelligence” too inclusive to be informative. Saying “everything is somewhat intelligent” can become a redefinition rather than a scientific discovery.

So the real disagreement isn’t mainly about the physics. Everyone agrees the behaviour is physically implemented. It’s about whether adaptive physical behaviour deserves the word **intelligence**, and what additional evidence would distinguish cognition from an ordinary self-organizing process.
